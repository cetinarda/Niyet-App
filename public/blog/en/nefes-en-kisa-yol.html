Body

Why is breath
the shortest way?

When you can't convince the mind, touch the body. Once the body calms down, the mind follows.

Sakin · 4 min read

Have you noticed that telling yourself “calm down” in an anxious moment doesn't work? There's a reason for it: in that moment, the mind trying to convince itself is that very same mind. An order coming out of the same noise won't quiet the noise.

But the body has another door.

You can't slow your heartbeat with your will. You can't run your digestion, your hormones, your immune system. But you can run your breath.

Breath is the only function of the autonomic nervous system that is both automatic and open to your will. It keeps going while you sleep, and yet you can take over whenever you like. That makes it a kind of control switch: the single wire running from the conscious side to the unconscious one.

What happens when you exhale?

There's a simple rule: the system speeds up as you breathe in and slows down as you breathe out. Your heart rate rises slightly on the in-breath and drops on the out-breath. That's why keeping your exhale longer than your inhale (in for 4, out for 8) is the most direct way to send your body the signal that “the danger has passed”.

It also explains why our breathing turns fast and shallow all by itself in a moment of panic. The body is getting ready to run. The trouble is that in modern life there is no lion to escape from; there's only an email, a conversation, an uncertainty. The body sounds the same alarm, but the alarm has nothing to answer to. And we get stuck right there, in that gap.

When you lengthen your breath, what you're doing is switching that alarm off by hand.

The ancient traditions already knew this

In Sanskrit the word for breath, prana, also means “life force”. Pranayama means the “expansion” or “guiding” of the breath, and in yoga it comes before meditation; because a settled mind is not expected from an unsettled breath.

In the Sufi tradition breath is a silent zikr repeated moment by moment: every breath taken in is a remembrance, every breath let out is a surrender. And in Zen the first instruction for sitting is always the same: “Just count your breaths.” Nothing to understand, nothing to solve, nothing to achieve. Only counting.

They all point to the same place: breath is the shortest door into the present moment. Because you cannot take your breath in the past or in the future.

So how does Sakin do this?

In Sakin, breath is not a single pattern. Because what you need isn't the same every day.

If you're tense, 4-7-8 (Letting Go) pulls the system down fast. If you need to focus, box breathing gathers the mind. If sleep won't come, Fall Asleep lengthens the exhale. If you feel scattered, Come to Center steadies you with an even rhythm. If you're tired, Renew brings you back to life. There are nine modes in all, and each one answers a different state.

On the screen you only see a ring that widens and narrows. You don't have to count or keep time, you just follow the ring. There's a voice guide too: in, hold, out, rest. You can close your eyes.

One minute is enough. Truly enough. Because the point isn't to correct your breathing; it's to open a small gap where you can say “I'm here” to your body.

Do you have a minute right now?

Three deep breaths, that's all. The rest slows down on its own.
